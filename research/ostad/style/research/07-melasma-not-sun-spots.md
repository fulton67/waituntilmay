## 2. Hook detail candidates (ranked)

1. **Melasma responds to visible light, not just UV. In a randomized trial, a tinted (iron-oxide) sunscreen beat a regular SPF 50 by 15% on top of the same prescription cream.** Castanedo-Cazares 2014. [PMID 24313385](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24313385/). Surprising, specific and actionable, and it corrects the plan's “mineral sunscreen” line.
2. **There is exactly one FDA-approved melasma cream, and it is labelled for eight weeks at a time.** FDA / Tri-Luma label. It makes “managed, not cured” concrete.
3. **Melasma's reported prevalence ranges from 1% to 50% depending on the population studied.** Ogbechie-Godec 2017. [PMID 28726212](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28726212/). Weak as a hook.

**Chosen: #1.** Consider opening on it: “Your sunscreen is blocking the wrong light.”

## 4. Do not say on camera

- That LUMA Cream (or any compounded cream) is FDA-approved, or imply it is related to Tri-Luma. The names are close; say “my compounded cream” and never “Luma” next to “FDA”.
- That a particular strength (6%, 8%) works better. There are no data, and higher strengths raise irritation and PIH risk.
- Hydroquinone as an OTC purchase (unapproved since the CARES Act, 2020; FDA warning letters April 2022). Long-term high-strength use carries a risk of exogenous ochronosis.
- Oral tranexamic acid as “FDA-approved for melasma.” It is approved for heavy menstrual bleeding; melasma use is off-label and needs a clotting-risk screen.
- “Cure.”
- “Mineral sunscreen” alone as sufficient (#7).
