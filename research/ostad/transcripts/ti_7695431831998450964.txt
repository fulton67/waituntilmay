Which of these four spots is a melanoma? Let's use dermoscopy to figure it out. Lesion 1 is a tiny flat, immensely pigmented, almost jet black macule. The degree of pigmentation can certainly look concerning. Dermoscopy shows a strikingly dark reticular pigment network that is sharply defined and mostly regular and organized, almost as if somebody dropped ink onto the skin. Diagnosis, ink spot lentigo. This one's benign. Lesion 2 is an irregularly shaped, deeply pigmented blue black macule on melanin rich skin, which clinically can certainly raise concern. Dermoscopy reveals a homogeneous structureless blue pigmentation. That blue colour likely represents melanin deeper in the dermis due to the Tyndall effect. Diagnosis: blue nevus. This one's also benign. Lesion 3, another dark, irregularly shaped macule. At First glance this almost looks like a pigment network but if you look closer, this is not a true melanocytic network. These are non melanocytic structures including fissures, ridges, milia like cysts and comedolike openings. Diagnosis, a benign sebaceous keratosis. With a naked eye, lesion 4 already has asymmetry, an irregular border, multiple colours including pink, brown and black and a growing diameter. On dermatoscopy, we can appreciate a true reticular melanocytic network at the periphery along with an architectural disorder, irregular pigmentation, areas of regression, irregular dots and globules, scar like depigmentation and areas of grey peppering. This is a melanoma in situ. Clinically pigmented lesions can look strikingly similar and even an expert eye may need a biopsy to confirm the Diagnosis, but dermoscopy brings us much closer to diagnostic certainty and helps us detect melanoma much earlier among its mimickers.
