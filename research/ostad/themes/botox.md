# Botox, baby Botox and the frozen-face fear

This theme is one long argument about when to start and how much is too much. One side says baby Botox is just smaller doses that soften fine lines and keep your face moving. The other says it is a marketing phrase built to hook women in their twenties, that it may dull how your face feeds emotion back to your brain, and that once you start you can't stop. Around that sit the practical fears: droopy eyelids, Botox that seems to stop working, and celebrities whose faces no longer move. People watch to find out whether they are too young, or already too frozen.

## The numbers

29 videos, 3% of the niche. 24% of them are winners and the median one does 2.72× its creator's usual; 66% (19 videos) are ones Dr. Ostad could make himself.

## What people claim

- Baby Botox means smaller doses that soften fine lines while keeping natural movement, and younger patients are asking for it (a Toronto clinic doctor, 49× usual).
- Preventive Botox in your early twenties may blunt the feedback your facial expressions send to your brain, so researchers advise against it (a German news piece, 29× usual).
- Baby Botox is a lie sold to women in their twenties to get them in sooner, and once you start you technically can't stop.
- Every experienced injector has caused eyelid ptosis; what matters is picking one who admits it and knows how to fix it.
- When Botox seems to stop working, it is usually handling, dilution, dose or ongoing aging; true antibody resistance is under 1%.
- A celebrity's sagging face is 24 years of normal aging, not Botox (an Indonesian doctor, 7.6× usual).
- Too much Botox has erased the expressions actresses need, with Nicole Kidman as the example.
- Masseter Botox can give you jowls, so one regular patient is pausing hers.
- A 27-year-old who asked for cheek filler needed masseter Jeuveau first, because clenching was widening her lower face.
- Full-face Botox across forehead, frown, crow's feet, lips, jaw and neck gives a fresh look without stiffness.
