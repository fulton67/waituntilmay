# Is it cancer? Spots, skin checks and Mohs

Everyone here is asking whether a spot is going to hurt them. Doctors answer with a quiz (“Is that skin cancer or not?”), the ABCDE checklist, or a dermoscope close-up of a 2 mm melanoma nobody would spot in the mirror. Patients answer with their own bodies: stage 4 melanoma symptoms, a mole that grew under a smartwatch, an eye swollen shut the morning after Mohs. The other half is the scar. People want to see how a mole comes off, whether Mohs will wreck their face, and what it looks like at four and six weeks. They watch to be reassured, and for the case they would have missed.

## What people claim

- A soft, raised mole is usually a harmless intradermal nevus; one that is asymmetric, has about three colours and is wider than 6 mm needs checking urgently.
- Early skin cancer often doesn't look like a mole at all: a “pimple” that never fully heals, a dry patch no moisturiser fixes, a tiny sore that bleeds when you wash your face.
- A melanoma can be 2 mm across and look normal to the naked eye; dermoscopy (“like an MRI for the skin”) is what catches it.
- Change matters more than looks. The spot that grows, changes colour or looks unlike your other moles (the “ugly duckling”) is the one to worry about.
- Clear Mohs slides aren't always the end: with an aggressive squamous cell cancer, an experienced surgeon keeps going and sends extra pathology to rule out spread along nerves.
- Mohs takes the cancer off in thin layers and reads each one under the microscope while you wait, quoted at a 98–99% cure rate while sparing healthy skin, which is why it's the standard for the nose, ears, lips and hands.
- Cosmetic moles come off three ways (laser, shave or excision), and the right one depends on the mole and where the scar will sit; an excision in the nasal crease hides the line.
- A good scar after mole removal is a routine, not luck: steri-strips for at least four weeks, silicone gel or tape, and sunscreen over it every day.
- A full-body skin check is quick and painless; come without makeup or nail polish, and expect the scalp, nails and tattoos to be checked.

## What the audience says back

The comments are personal and anxious: viewers say moles that fit the warning signs were dismissed by their own doctors, admit to health anxiety, and one who lost her mother to melanoma asks for an ABCDE video. Under the stage 4 warning-signs video, survivors and a widower share their stories, some say they had no symptoms at all, and others worry their symptoms overlap with menopause. The run-1 questions add two sharper ones: “how deadly is skin cancer?” and a viewer who asks why a basal cell that is “almost never deadly” needs Mohs at all, calling it “almost … a scam”.
