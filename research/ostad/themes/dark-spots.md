# Dark spots, melasma and skin of color

This theme is mostly people with brown and Black skin trying to fade marks that won't leave: acne dark spots, dark underarms and elbows, and melasma that stayed after pregnancy. Influencers sell the fix (a kojic soap, a TXA serum, “results in 30 days”). Doctors mostly warn: don't do a 30% TCA peel at home, a $1,000 spa peel without prep can burn darker skin, and melasma isn't ordinary dark spots, so sunscreen and vitamin C alone won't clear it. Under all of it runs a fear of being burned or ignored by clinics that don't know dark skin. People watch for a before-and-after they can believe.

## What people claim

- Never do a 30% TCA peel at home: it's a medium-to-deep peel that can burn and scar, and nobody with a medium to deep skin tone should try it.
- Laser can fade acne dark spots on Black skin if the strength is kept low; that means more sessions, and it also cuts the facial hair that causes ingrown-hair spots.
- Melasma is not the same as dark spots. It's driven by sun, hormones and heat, so one peel or one sunscreen won't clear it, and med-spa IPL can make it worse.
- Hydroquinone is the gold standard for melasma, but you have to cycle it, two to three months on and two to three months off.
- A spa's $1,000 peels without proper prep burned a client with skin of colour, and now the hyperpigmentation has to be treated.
- Pigmentation in brown and Black skin gets worse with too little sunscreen, no hat, harsh acids and lasers (one derm's view; others argue the right laser is safe).
- Darkness on the body that lasts longer than six weeks isn't a tan, it's pigmentation, and you can't scrub it away.
- Dark underarms are mostly friction from repeated shaving.
- Melasma is partly a blood-vessel problem, which is why it keeps coming back.
- Skin isn't meant to be one even colour; darker elbows, knuckles and skin around the mouth are normal biology.
- Only about 3% of US dermatologists are Black, which is why people struggle to find one who understands their skin.

## How it sounds

Selling is the loudest tone (33%), followed by confessional diaries (23%) and clinical explainers (23%), with fear in a fifth (20%). ## What the audience says back

Under a nurse's laser series for hyperpigmentation on Black skin, commenters defend that she is evening the skin tone, not lightening it, and ask whether it hurts and whether it works on lighter skin. Under a body-routine video, viewers joke about the cost and ask whether kojic soap can be an everyday body wash. The run-1 questions repeat the same worry: “how is this on brown skin that is prone to hyperpigmetation?”, “Effective on lighter skin as well?” (461 likes), and two separate askers wanting to know if a treatment works for melasma. One of the most-watched videos here (598×) is just a mother asking strangers for help with pregnancy melasma her doctor said would fade.
