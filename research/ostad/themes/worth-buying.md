# What's worth buying

This theme is about money. Dermatologists film "things I'd never spend money on" and "things I'd splurge on" lists: La Mer, Nutrafol, ice rollers, £1,000 LED masks and collagen gummies get trashed, while a retinoid, SkinCeuticals CE Ferulic, Botox and lasers get defended. Underneath runs one argument: med-spa nurses and product reps say "medical grade" means stronger actives, while dermatologists and creators say the term is unregulated and Vanicream works fine. Much of the reach is sponsored derms selling La Roche-Posay, Medik8 or Medicube. People watch so they don't waste money, and to hear a doctor name a brand.

## The numbers

57 videos, 6% of the niche. Only 16% of them are winners and the median one does 0.99× its creator's usual (0.96× for the ones he could make); 67% (38 videos) are videos Dr. Ostad could make himself.

## What people claim

- As a dermatologist I'd never pay for La Mer, Nutrafol or facials; I'd spend that money on lasers, peels and microneedling.
- Many viral products are overpriced or irritating; a drugstore pick often beats a £280 moisturizer, and nobody needs a niacinamide that strong.
- Ice rollers, £1,000 LED masks, collagen gummies and face-yoga gadgets aren't worth the money.
- The products worth paying for are a retinoid, SkinCeuticals CE Ferulic and a good acid toner.
- Detoxing your skin isn't real, packaging only matters for vitamin C, and coconut oil and beef tallow are bad for the face.
- Stop buying skincare until you have a cleanser, a moisturizer and a sunscreen; daily sunscreen users showed 24% less skin aging over four and a half years.
- "Medical grade" isn't regulated by the FDA, so any brand can call itself that tomorrow.
- Medical-grade skincare has higher concentrations of actives and improves the results of lasers and injectables.
- The best sunscreen is the one you'll wear daily; a higher price mostly buys texture and tint.
- The treatments worth a derm's own money are a neuromodulator, a resurfacing laser and something tightening.

## How it sounds

Doctors make 56% of it, influencers 25% and nurses or injectors 9%. The tone is casual and friendly (39%), salesy (33%) and contrarian (30%): mostly younger derm-influencers being blunt about brands ("I hate to do a product brand call out"). The winning hook is a list that puts the credential and the money in the first sentence: "things I would never spend money on as a board certified dermatologist". List opens are the single most common hook (23%).

Three opening lines that worked:

> “Three things that I would never spend money on as a board certified dermatologist.” (@dralidempsey, 82× usual)
> “Things I don't care about as a dermatologist.” (@thebeautyderm, 8.4× usual)
> “Things that are not worth your money from a cosmetic dermatologist.” (@drsomji, 7.9× usual)

## What the audience says back

People argue back. Under the "not worth your money" list, viewers defend their LED masks, complain that dermatologists contradict each other, and point out the LED-mask ads sitting in the same comments. Under the skit calling "medical grade" an unregulated term, they recommend CeraVe and The Ordinary, and some defend SkinCeuticals while agreeing the label guarantees nothing. Run 1's questions ask the same thing in different words: "So LED masks and collagen tablets aren't worth it?!", "what about collagen powder?", a budget-friendly retinoid (1,116 likes) and "How much does something like this cost?"

CEG + Ferulic is his direct answer to the CE Ferulic every derm here praises, and he can say plainly where his version differs and where the drugstore is fine. - **The trap:** the med-spa pitch that "medical grade is stronger, buy it from us". It is the losing side of this argument, made by nurses, reps and estheticians at 0.06× to 2.3×, and the other side tells viewers to find a new provider if theirs says drugstore products are bad. A Park Avenue derm calling drugstore skincare useless while selling $760 sets walks straight into that. Hauls and dollar-store reviews are off-brand for him too.
