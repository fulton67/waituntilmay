# Salmon sperm, exosomes and copper peptides

This theme is the Korean-import gold rush. Influencers rub on PDRN creams, film salmon sperm facials, say an exosome serum replaced their whole ten-step routine, and promise copper peptides will give the glow looksmaxxers have, usually with a discount link. The gross-out word does half the work. A small group of doctors and pharmacists push back: PDRN on bare skin does nothing, there is little evidence topical exosomes get in, the "copper uglies" are just irritation, and you should ask where your exosomes come from. People watch for the novelty and the glass-skin promise, and some for the sceptic who tells them what to throw away.

## The numbers

64 videos, 7% of the niche. Only 8% of them are winners and the median one does 0.72× its creator's usual, but the makeable ones do better at 1.23×; just 31% (20 videos) are ones Dr. Ostad could make himself.

## What people claim

- A Korean salmon-DNA facial with microneedling leaves skin firmer and more luminous within a week (an influencer's test, 397× usual).
- An exosome serum can replace your entire skincare routine.
- Copper peptide (GHK-Cu) rubbed on works as well as the injected kind, and cleared one creator's acne scars.
- A PDRN cream can stand in for Botox, plumping and smoothing tired skin.
- If you only apply PDRN on your skin, throw it away: it needs microneedle channels to get in (a Korean pharmacist, 18× usual).
- Exosomes are little packets of growth factors that tell your cells to repair, usually done as a series of three treatments.
- There is no good evidence that PDRN in an eye cream penetrates enough to do anything meaningful.
- Topical exosomes are among the most overhyped ingredients, with no reliable evidence they get through the skin.
- The "copper uglies" aren't real: redness and peeling after copper peptides mean irritation, so stop.
- Not all exosome treatments are equal: ask where they come from, because source, quality and regulation vary.

## How it sounds

Influencers carry more than half of it (55%), with doctors at 17% and brands at 14%, and more than half the tone is pure sales; sponsored posts are the single biggest genre. Much of it is lip-sync or silent clips with a product in hand. The hooks that win are curiosity and disgust: "salmon sperm", a Korean secret, or a peptide every looksmaxxer supposedly uses. The sceptics who do well here open by telling you to stop or throw something away.

## What the audience says back

Two clips came with comments, and they show both moods. Under the "exosomes replaced my routine" video, commenters object that human exosomes come from placentas without consent and warn that they are unregulated and may raise cancer risk, while a few recommend Korean exosome lines. Under a man getting salmon DNA injected in Korea, viewers are shocked and grossed out, joke about fertilizing, and some admit he is glowing. The run 1 questions point the same way: "So LED masks and collagen tablets aren't worth it?!" and "Okay but what about collagen powder?". People want to know which of these is real.
