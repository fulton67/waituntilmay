# The derm as entertainer

This is the niche's sideshow: cysts drained in close-up over pop music, patients babbling after anesthesia, dermatologists lip-syncing to trend sounds, staff skits, a meme about lip filler that "migrated", and a large share that isn't dermatology at all (celebrity edits, chiropractic cracks, anime recaps that matched the name Ariel). Most of it makes no claim; the point is a gasp or a laugh. The few that teach do it as a reaction, a dermatologist duetting someone else's clip to say the morning-shed routine is staged or the extraction tool is pushed too hard. People watch to be grossed out, to laugh, or to see a doctor be human.

## The numbers

93 videos, 10% of the niche. 17% of them are winners and the median one does 0.98× its creator's usual (0.99× for the ones he could make); only 24% are videos Dr. Ostad could make himself, and nearly all of those depend on his own extraction footage or on reacting to someone else's clip.

## What people claim

Most of these videos claim nothing. The ones that do:

- Lip filler doesn't dissolve on its own, it migrates (a meme, 25×).
- "Morning shed" routines are staged: nobody sleeps like that, and facial spray over a silicone mask does nothing (1.9×).
- Extreme prosthetic-makeup clips are rage bait, and the perfect finish was probably wiped off and redone (2.0×).
- Most people press too hard with a comedone extractor; counter-pressure from the other side, or two cotton swabs, causes less trauma.
- Eyelid twitching is myokymia from stress, sleep loss or too much caffeine, and rest, warm compresses and time fix it.
- Slathering your face in ointment before bed ("face basting") is worth your family's stares (a comedy clip, 51×).
- "Skin longevity" and ageless red-carpet skin are the goal (an Oscars post with no speech, 9.7×).
- There is no single "secret" behind Indian women's thick hair, and generalising about a whole group is wrong.

## How it sounds

Humor is 58% of the tone. Doctors front about half of it (48%), but accounts that aren't dermatologists at all front another quarter (26%), and six of the ten biggest results belong to them. The winners rarely open with a sentence: the top video is a cyst on screen with music over it (686×), the second an anesthesiologist singing (333×), so the hook is a curiosity gap (27%), a reaction (22%) or a gross-out (12%). The one repeatable spoken pattern is the reaction stitch: "Hi, dermatologist here," then running commentary on someone else's video.

Three opening lines that worked:

> “tell me your lip fillers migrated without telling me your lip fillers migrated” (@ruzannasmbatyan6739, 25×)
> “Hi. Dermatologist here. Thanks to whoever tagged me.” (@dermangelo, 2.0×)
> “Hi, dermatologist here to remind you that all of this morning shed content” (@dermangelo, 1.9×)

## What the audience says back

Only three videos have comment data, and all three show viewers policing whether something is real or appropriate. Under a staged clip of a patient hearing he has cancer, viewers asked why it was recorded, called the doctor unprofessional and suspected it was staged. Under a PCOS weight-loss reveal, commenters argued about "normalizing taking ozempic for non-essential purposes" (a run-1 question with 1,657 likes). Under an off-topic lip-sync, viewers found it creepy until others explained the pair were siblings.

## Ostad's angle

- **Maps to:** very little, honestly; most of this theme is off-limits for him. What does map is the procedures that produce footage people stop for: excisions, mole removal, scar revision, earlobe repair, and Mohs and tumescent liposuction done under local anesthesia, where the patient is awake and can talk.
- **Only he can say:** The narrow slice he can use is (1) his own procedure footage with consenting patients, framed by a surgeon who explains what you are looking at rather than just playing music over it; (2) awake-patient reactions from procedures done under local anesthesia, the honest opposite of the "funny things people say after anesthesia" compilations; and (3) a dry, deadpan reaction format, a surgeon who has taught NYU residents for years watching a bad extraction or a staged routine and saying in one line what is wrong with it.
- **The trap:** A triple-board-certified surgeon in his 50s selling a "discerning clientele" on natural results gains nothing from a lip-sync, and his own two posts here (an "on this day" repost at 876 views, a Jeuveau Times Square billboard clip at 335) show it. Avoid lip-syncs, trend sounds, staff dance skits, memes, mocking patients ("You need to stop thinking that you're ugly you are"), politics and off-topic posts, and filming a patient at a vulnerable moment, which is exactly what drew "unprofessional" in the comments. Plain pimple-popping without his voice would also make the practice look like an extraction facial shop.
