# The sunscreen fight

This is an argument, not a tutorial. One side says sunscreen is toxic, chemical filters soak into your blood, some sun is good for you and the cancers it causes are generally not the deadliest ones. The other side, mostly dermatologists, answers that the sun is the number one proven cause of skin cancer, that sunscreen doesn't cause it, that vitamin D survives real-world use and that there is no safe tan. Wedged in between are sponsored launches from ISDIN, La Roche-Posay and Shiseido. People watch to see who wins, and because a melanoma survivor or a sarcastic derm makes it personal.

## The numbers

21 videos, 2% of the niche. Only 14% of them are winners and the median one does 1.26× its creator's usual, falling to 0.80× for the videos Dr. Ostad could make; 67% (14 videos) are ones he could make himself, and he has made none so far.

## What people claim

- Some sun is good for brain and body, and the skin cancers the sun causes are mostly not the deadly kind (a podcast clip, the top performer at 66×).
- Chemical sunscreen filters show up in the blood after a single application and linger for weeks, so mineral is the safer choice (2.9M views).
- Sunscreen does not cause cancer: the scare comes from absorption studies and benzene-contaminated aerosol batches, and a review of 29 studies covering 313,000 people found no link.
- Studies that tie sunscreen users to more skin cancer are correlation: the people who wear the most sunscreen are the ones in the sun the most.
- Real-world sunscreen use has little to no effect on vitamin D levels.
- There's no such thing as a safe tan; a base tan is sun damage, and everyone needs sunscreen however dark their skin.
- Mineral isn't automatically better for hyperpigmentation on Black skin; broad spectrum, enough of it, and an iron-oxide tint for melasma matter more.
- UV gets through clouds and windows, so wear it in winter and at your desk, and reapply every two hours to get the SPF on the label.
- No study shows daily sunscreen lowers your chance of dying of skin cancer, and diligent use hasn't changed basal cell rates (a Los Angeles skin cancer surgeon, at 0.05×).

## How it sounds

Contrarian is the default (67%) on both sides, with fear in 29% and open selling in 19%. Doctors are most of the speakers (57%), then influencers (24%). The dominant hook is a contrarian claim stated as fact in the first line (38%), but the videos that did best did something else: a podcast guest framing anti-sunscreen as what “most” derms quietly believe, a dermatologist playing an anti-sunscreen convert so straight that viewers thought she meant it, and a derm rating Met Gala looks by UV coverage. The earnest one-by-one myth-busts mostly land under their creators' usual.

## What the audience says back

Under the sarcastic anti-sunscreen video, viewers say they were briefly scared she meant it, some still ask whether they should wear sunscreen, and others warn that people are missing the joke. The run-1 questions show what people actually want, and it isn't the culture war: “I'm confused. Should we wear sunscreen or not?” (1,820 likes), whether a retinoid user still needs daily SPF (1,789), whether arms and other exposed skin need it every day (631), and how reapplying works over makeup (209). The confusion is practical, and nobody in this theme is answering it plainly.
