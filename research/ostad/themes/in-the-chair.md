# In the chair: PRP, microneedling and small procedures

This theme is about what the appointment actually looks like. Blood gets spun in a centrifuge and injected back under the eyes, a pen drags across a numbed face, veins vanish under a needle, seven lipomas come out on a lunch break. The talking is mostly practical: how many sessions, what you can't put on your skin for four days, how long the sunburn look lasts. The warnings are sharper: vampire facials at an unlicensed spa passed on HIV, and PRP is wrong for people with cancer history or blood disorders. People watch for the footage, and to decide whether they could sit through it.

## The numbers

23 videos, 2% of the niche. Only 13% of them are winners and the median one does 0.74× its creator's usual (0.71× for the videos Dr. Ostad could make); 78% (18 videos) are ones he could make himself. This is a weak theme overall, carried by a couple of outliers.

## What people claim

- PRP is made by drawing your blood and spinning it twice, then injecting the plasma into smile lines or thinning brows.
- Under-eye PRP looks wild straight afterwards, but the swelling settles within 24 hours.
- What you do after microneedling matters more than the treatment: leave the serum on for at least 12 hours, no actives, retinoids or hard workouts at first, then bring them back over days four to seven.
- Microneedling is a slow collagen game: you won't see an overnight change, so cap it at three or four sessions a year, and PRP only helps some people.
- PRP plus microneedling once a month for four sessions, then two or three times a year, improves acne scars, pores and tone in all skin types.
- The CDC linked HIV cases to vampire facials at a New Mexico spa, so PRP, Botox and filler belong only in a doctor's clinic.
- PRP isn't for everyone: not with a cancer history, an active infection, active autoimmune disease or a blood or platelet disorder.
- SkinPen microneedling is tolerable, with five to seven days of sunburn-like downtime that peaks on days one to three.
- Lipomas can come out under local anaesthetic in a lunch break, with a week off the gym.
- Ellacor removes tiny cores of skin, about 0.4 mm wide and up to 8% of the skin, to tighten the jowls without surgery (Dr. Ostad's own explainer, reposted by the brand).

## How it sounds

Clinical (35%) and salesy (30%), with a casual, come-with-me register (26%) from influencers and injectors. Doctors speak in 43% of these, but nurses and injectors (17%), influencers (17%) and brands (13%) make up most of the rest, so it often plays like a clinic ad. The dominant hook is the procedure itself in close-up (visual shock, 22%) or a POV that puts you in the chair (17%). The two biggest winners are a wordless PRP-microneedling clip and a doctor narrating a PRP prep step by step from the first line.

Three opening lines that worked:

## What the audience says back

Under a viral microneedling-with-PRP clip for acne scars, viewers ask about the risk of scarring, whether it works on brown skin prone to hyperpigmentation, what the aftercare is, what it costs and how long it lasts, and one says it went badly for them. Under Dr. Ostad's own Ellacor explainer, viewers ask whether it works on neck laxity and how many sessions it takes, and several say the short downtime is what decides it for them. The run-1 questions are the same practical list: cost, “How often would you need to keep doing this”, purging after microneedling, and microneedling versus HydraFacial.
